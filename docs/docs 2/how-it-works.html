Under the hood

How the scam detector works

Lantern Guide doesn't send your messages to an outside AI company. It uses its own machine-learning model, trained by Max on more than 26,000 text messages, to sort each message into normal, junk or scam, and then explains its answer in plain words.

From message to answer

What happens when you check a message

Read the message

Paste a text or email, or upload a screenshot. Screenshots are turned into text first.

Clean it up

Links, emails and phone numbers become placeholders, and disguises like "FR33" or "c.l.a.i.m" are undone.

Two models read it

A fast word-pattern model and a RoBERTa language model each estimate how likely it is to be normal, junk or a scam.

Combine and decide

Their answers are blended, checked against tuned thresholds and red-flag rules, then explained in everyday words.

The two models

Two different ways of reading

Scammers change their wording all the time. Using two models that read in different ways makes the detector harder to fool.

Classic model: word patterns

Turns each message into thousands of word and letter-pattern features (TF-IDF on word pairs and 3–5 letter chunks), then uses logistic regression to estimate each class. Three classic models were compared (logistic regression, naive Bayes and a support vector machine), and logistic regression scored best. It's fast and small, and good at spotting scam vocabulary.

RoBERTa: reading in context

A transformer language model (roberta-base) fine-tuned on the training messages on a GPU in Google Colab. It reads whole sentences in context, so it can recognize a scam even when it's worded in a new way. Training used class weights, label smoothing and early stopping.

The blend

70% classic30% RoBERTa

Blends from 10% to 90% classic were tested on validation messages. 70/30 scored best at separating scams and junk from normal messages (precision-recall AUC).

The training data

Good answers start with good data

26,395text messages
5sources: four public datasets (2011–2025) plus modern examples
823wrong labels corrected after review
400test messages labeled blind
  • Cleaning the labels. About 18% of the newest dataset's "scams" were really ordinary messages, like verification codes and delivery updates. 2,224 suspicious labels were reviewed: 823 were corrected and 501 unclear messages were removed.
  • A fair test. Messages were split by scam campaign into training (19,754), validation (3,308) and test (3,333), so copies of the same scam never appear in both training and testing.
  • A trustworthy score. 400 test messages were labeled blind, without seeing the dataset's label, so the final score doesn't depend on mistakes in the original data.
  • Learning to see through tricks. Some training messages got disguised copies (number-for-letter swaps, look-alike letters, dots, odd capitals). Three other tricks were kept secret and used only to stress-test the model on disguises it had never seen.

The label corrections and the 400 blind labels were made with the help of an AI reviewer and spot-checked by Max.

Making the call

Honest answers, including "I'm not sure"

Instead of one yes/no cutoff, Lantern Guide uses two thresholds, both set on validation messages it never trained on.

Looks normal

Below both thresholds.

Not sure

In between: "check with a family member before replying."

Scam or junk

Above the warning threshold, set so that at most about 1% of normal messages get a warning.

  • Red-flag rules catch things real companies essentially never ask for by text, like gift cards, remote access to your computer or your PIN. A strong red flag turns a borderline answer into a warning.
  • Link checks compare where links really go with the company the message claims to be from, catching look-alikes like "paypa1.com." Messages that only link to a company's real website get a calmer answer.
  • Plain-words reasons. Every verdict comes with reasons, like "It asks you to pay with gift cards," and what to do next.
